Neural networks are routinely caught doing something that looks impossible:
packing more features into a layer than the layer has dimensions. This trick is
called **superposition**, and it works because features are sparse — most of
them are silent most of the time, so they can share space and only rarely step
on each other. But sharing space means that sometimes two features *do* speak at
once, their signals add up, and someone downstream has to untangle the mess.
Which way of packing handles those collisions best? That is an open research
question — and this little game puts you in the network's shoes.

## The rules

You play two roles, at two moments in time. First, before deployment, you
**program a remote weather station** — you decide how it will encode its
measurements. Then the station is shipped far away, and you sit at headquarters
**decoding whatever it sends back**.

- The station carries four instruments — a thermometer, a hygrometer, a
  barometer and an anemometer — each rescaled to read 0–100.
- Each round, every instrument independently *wakes up* with 20% probability;
  an instrument that sleeps reads 0. (Rounds where all four sleep are skipped.)
- The station sits in a remote spot with a desperately weak signal — each round
  the link has bandwidth for exactly **one vector**, which appears as a single
  arrow on the 2D display at headquarters. Your program assigns each instrument
  its own arrow on that plane; the station automatically transmits the sum of
  those arrows, each scaled by its instrument's reading.
- The link is weak and the display is coarse — it resolves the received vector
  only to the nearest 0.1 in each coordinate.
- At headquarters you see only the received vector. Recover all four readings;
  every miss costs its square, so one big miss hurts more than many small ones.
  Five rounds, lowest total wins.
- **The Oracle** — a perfect decoder that computes the exact best possible
  guess from your program — sits next to you at headquarters and decodes every
  message too. Even the Oracle pays for ambiguity: whenever two different
  combinations of awake instruments send the same arrow, hedging is all anyone
  can do. That unavoidable loss is your program's **confusion floor**, shown
  live while you design.
- The station's factory setting is the naive program: two antipodal pairs on a
  cross. When two paired instruments wake up together, their contributions
  collide on the same line. **Reprogram the arrows, shrink the floor, and try
  to out-decode the Oracle.**
